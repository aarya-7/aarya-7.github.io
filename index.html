Churn & Retention Analytics

SaaS Churn Early-Warning System

RavenStack, a synthetic SaaS company, needed to know not just that churn was happening, but where it was concentrated and whether a simple, explainable warning system could actually catch it before the fact.

Working in PostgreSQL, I built cohort retention tracking (M1/M3/M6) to find where the drop-off actually starts, quantified churned MRR by segment to find where the dollars were leaking from, and validated a rules-based risk-banding system — HIGH/MEDIUM/LOW — against real churn outcomes rather than just theorizing about which signals should matter.

The risk bands held up under validation: HIGH-risk accounts churned at 50%, more than 3x the MEDIUM-risk rate, with usage drop and error rate emerging as the earliest, most reliable signals — ahead of anything from support tickets or billing. The whole thing was packaged into executive-facing Tableau dashboards, so the answer to "who's at risk and why" didn't require a SQL query every time someone asked.

Funnel & Conversion Analysis

E-Commerce Funnel Diagnostics

The instinct going in was that checkout would be the leak — it usually is. Working from the RetailRocket eCommerce events dataset (views, add-to-cart, transactions), I built a session-level clickstream mart in PostgreSQL from scratch: sessionization, timestamp cleaning, and stage-by-stage conversion tracking across the full funnel.

The data told a different story. View-to-cart converted at just 2.21% — a 97.79% drop — while cart-to-purchase actually held up fine at 27.17%. Aggregate, top-line metrics would have hidden this entirely; only session-level tracking surfaced exactly where the funnel actually breaks.

That reframed the fix: not a checkout redesign, but product-page clarity. I also found returning visitors converting at nearly 3x the rate of new visitors during peak hours, pointing at the first-visit experience as much as the funnel itself. The output was 3 proposed A/B tests (PDP clarity, category UX, cart recovery) and 4 Tableau dashboards, packaged into a stakeholder memo — not just a chart, a specific, arguable recommendation.

Anomaly Detection · Python

GMV Anomaly Detection Pipeline

Revenue swings are normal in GMV data — the hard part is telling a real anomaly apart from ordinary volatility fast enough for it to matter. Standard deviation-based thresholds kept getting fooled by that volatility, throwing false alarms or missing the real ones.

I switched to MAD-based robust z-scores on detrended residuals, which held up cleanly against 3 synthetic stress-test shocks where the standard-deviation approach didn't. The pipeline runs in Python against a PostgreSQL revenue store, decomposing trend from noise before scoring, then tagging each flagged event with a severity level and a driver attribution — which region, which segment, how much of the delta it explains.

The last piece was making the output usable without a follow-up meeting: I constrained an LLM to only the pre-computed fact packets (no open-ended querying), so every generated brief is auditable back to a specific number. Across 18 months, the pipeline surfaced 12 flagged events this way — including a Black Friday spike of +558% (z=29.79) correctly attributed to Southeast Brazil at 72% of the total delta, with zero manual digging.

Data Engineering · dbt/Snowflake

H-1B LCA Disclosure Analytics Pipeline

This one started as a data engineering exercise and became a question worth answering: how is H-1B sponsorship actually distributed across employers, industries, and roles, especially in the NY/NJ tech market?

The pipeline moves DOL's quarterly LCA disclosure Excel/Parquet files into Snowflake, then through dbt in explicit layers — RAW (an untouched mirror of the source), staging (rename/cast only), intermediate (union all 4 quarters, dedupe to one row per case), and marts (wage-normalized, ready for BI). Every schema name signals exactly what's been touched, so nothing gets quietly patched in the warehouse.

The real work was in the edge cases the source data doesn't hand you cleanly: Q1's raw schema uses a hyphenated column name that Q2–Q4 don't, dates arrive as nanosecond Unix timestamps instead of real dates, and wages mix Hour/Week/Bi-Weekly/Month/Year units in the same column. I normalized all of it to an annualized figure in the marts layer — and where a value was implausible but plausibly just mislabeled, I flagged it in a separate column instead of silently correcting or dropping it. The goal throughout: every transformation decision stays visible and defensible, not buried.

Applied ML · Fraud Detection

Credit Card Fraud Detection

Fraud detection is a textbook imbalanced-classification problem — the interesting cases are a tiny fraction of the data, which makes accuracy a useless metric; a model that never flags fraud is still "99.8% accurate."

Working from the anonymized, PCA-transformed Kaggle credit card transaction dataset, I benchmarked three classifiers — Random Forest, AdaBoost, and XGBoost — scored by ROC-AUC instead. XGBoost led clearly at 0.982, ahead of Random Forest (0.902) and AdaBoost (0.870).

Because the features are PCA components rather than raw transaction fields, there's no way to reason about them from domain knowledge alone — the exploratory analysis had to lean entirely on the data's statistical structure to find what actually separated fraud from legitimate activity. I built interactive Plotly visualizations around the results so the class-imbalance tradeoffs and model comparisons were something a reader could actually inspect, not just a single accuracy number to take on faith.
